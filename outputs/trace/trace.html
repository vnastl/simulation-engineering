Interviewer: I’ll tell you a little bit about the project to begin with and the questions I’ll be asking. Well, basically I’m looking at how the industrial placement year affects your career decisions and whether you still want to be an engineer and we’re trying to look at ways that we can get women to stay in engineering long-term.
Subject: OK.
Interviewer: Can you just remind me what course you’re doing?
Subject: Mechanical engineering.
Interviewer: Right, OK and what A levels did you do?
Subject: Er, physics, chemistry, with AS level Maths and Theatre Studies.
Interviewer: Ok, how old are you?
Subject: 19.
Interviewer: 19 – did you come straight from doing A levels to university then?
Subject: Yes.
Interviewer: What type of school did you go to?
Subject: Girl’s Grammar School.
Interviewer: Right and have you got any previous work experience?
Subject: Er, I did a Nuffield bursary for 4 weeks with [company 1] and last time I worked with [company 2].
Interviewer: Right, OK – what do your parents do?
Subject: Er my mother’s a nurse and my dad is a self-support engineer.
Interviewer: OK, I’m just going to ask you now, a few things about how you decided to go into engineering really. So, when and why did you decide to enter engineering?
Subject: Erm, I was always interested in Maths and Physics and I did my – how theoretical physics was – so I started looking into engineering (OK) see if that was suited better.
Interviewer: OK, what factors do you think influenced your decision – like family and education and your strengths? Like why did you decide to do the Maths and Physics and maybe not Sociology or something like that?
Subject: Erm, I’ve always been very practical. I mean I can guild numbers – anything with key words, or trying to fill up 2 sides of A4 when it only needs half a side, to be precise (Mm) I’ve just never been able to do. And it’s the same kind of thing – both of my parents are the same way, but yeah.
Interviewer: OK. Did you know any engineers to talk to? Did you say your dad was an engineer?
Subject: Erm, he’s sales support – he’s – he doesn’t – he’s not an engineer he just runs the technical side of the sales, proves that it’s not illegal (yeah) or dangerous to build it. He doesn’t do any actual engineering.
Interviewer: He’s just in the industry?
Subject: Yeah, he used to be an apprentice, he did an apprenticeship with [company]Forecast to be an engineer, but it closed down – he stopped doing that.
Interviewer: OK, what kind of things appeal to you about engineering? Is it the practical?
Subject: The practical side and being able to see something – even design something – you’re likely to see it come out the end (yeah) or be able to go and run a test and see the final result, and see it go out to manufacture. (Yeah) It just brings the whole process really of being able to do things – different parts of the process, rather than being stuck doing one or the other. You can see so much further.
Interviewer: Yes, so it’s quite a satisfying feeling – project – see it all.
Subject: Yeah.
Interviewer: OK, what was it that specifically attracted you to mechanical engineering? Was there a reason that you didn’t want to study say Civil engineering?
Subject: Erm, I hadn’t ever really considered doing civil and things like that, because it never really got talked about. When I was looking into engineering it was more aero or auto mechanical systems. Those were the only ones that got talked about and I didn’t want to specify – as much to do – just aero or auto. And, er, mechanical was just – it was varied enough for you to get as much out of it as you wanted to, regarding – to varying a module and everything and not knowing much about it, within the (?) of doing it. So it seemed a better choice.
Interviewer: What sort of research did you do? You said that civil and that weren’t really talked about it (lost words)?
Subject: It was things like erm, the careers officer at school, the insight courses I went on – it was never really, they just went, and if they did do things outside those four it was normally chemical engineering, and all the materials engineering and then things like that.
Interviewer: Have your family been supportive of your choices?
Subject: Er my Dad was, my mum wasn’t.
Interviewer: She wanted you to do something else?
Subject: She thought I should be doing Maths or Accounting, because she thought there was better job prospects and the course wouldn’t be as heavy, so I’d find it easier.
Interviewer: Was it difficult for you with her not supporting you?
Subject: Well, not so much now, it was in the first year, because if I had any problems or if – there were some weeks all the coursework was due in, I was having a panic, it became I told you not to do the course and things like that, which was a bit hard but now she’s kind of accepted the fact I’m here whether she likes it or not.
Interviewer: OK, you say you went to Careers people (yeah) were they supportive?
Subject: Er, yeah. Our school was, at the time, trying to go for a Beacon school in Science and things like that and I kind of became their token engineer for the year – because they kind of said, because they always tried to get and have – well always ended up having an engineer, a doctor and I can’t remember what the other one is –but either way it’s kind– that kind of thing, so when they thought I might be interested in doing it (yeah) they backed me up a lot for it, so …
Interviewer: Did you feel a bit like they pushed you too much sometimes or didn’t you mind that?
Subject: No, I quite liked it because a lot of the other people who were trying to find out about what they wanted to do, were having a lot of trouble getting hold of information and things, although I was kind of given all the help I wanted to because the Careers people were really into it, my Physics teacher was really excited about it and things like that.
Interviewer: OK, are you going on a placement?
Subject: Hopefully, I haven’t had any responses yet.
Interviewer: Right, OK. What do you think it was that made you want to do a Sandwich course?
Subject: Erm, well firstly because 4 week – 4 years, straight through University seemed like a lot to take on really (yes) because I’ve always wanted to do things like placement stuff, even if it was just to see which bits of engineering I didn’t want to do. (Yeah) So, it’s kind of knowing where I want to go when I’m finished. Gain the experience and just seeing how it does work in the real world, as opposed to design this and you have a lathe and this. And knowing what’s out there.
Interviewer: OK. How did you decide to come to [this] University?
Subject: Erm, it was mainly through erm league tables and things because it kept coming up as something I should look at, and erm, yeah, when I came to apply it was looking for the right grades, so I signed up.
Interviewer: OK. When you decided that you were interested in engineering did you realise that it was so male dominated?
Subject: Yeah, but I’ve never really seen that as a problem. (OK) It’s one of these things that people keep quoting to me and trying – well you both doing your job – as long as they do their job and get on with it. I’m not bothered, at all.
Interviewer: Do you think that more women should be encouraged into engineering?
Subject: Erm, I don’t know. Most of the girls I knew and grew up with, and like I’m meeting now (yeah) they wouldn’t fit in with it. They don’t tend to like – they aren’t people who have done physics stuff, - the people who are more into things like computing and stuff like that. It would be a nice idea but I don’t think many people – it’s not just that they aren’t being aware of it, as an option, they don’t really want to be doing it and – because they’re kind of happy with the idea of doing design and things, it’s just the idea that comes with it, of having to put on overalls and fiddle with the stuff. And they just don’t see it as something they’d like to do, the same way that a lot of the blokes I know wouldn’t go into it because they don’t like that kind of thing.
Interviewer: Do you think that the reason people might think they don’t want to do it is based on true ideas or false perceptions about what it is really like?
Subject: I think they’re partially true ideas because – but they can get overtaken a lot. Sometimes people do see it as getting down, getting something, stripping it apart or rebuilding it. And they kind of miss the fact that its not just that, you’re doing – like on the chemicals you’re doing fluids and you’re doing design work and all the rest of things. 99% of the time you’re behind a computer and like one module every year, I think, is actually taking stuff apart, so…
Interviewer: OK, what characteristics do you think makes a good engineer? If you had to describe an ideal engineer?
Subject: Er, don’t know – analytical, needing to find out how things work – just curious about everything, I think, just so that you know you can design something, you know, look at it and actually go no I can make it better or – why not do this instead and all the rest of it.
Interviewer: And you said earlier that you didn’t think it mattered whether you’re a male or a female, but do you think that there are any advantages or disadvantages to being a woman engineer?
Subject: Erm, not really. Erm, but then again some people, you know, on placements were saying that because there aren’t enough women engineers, people are more likely to give us jobs. And a lot of the blokes are – and it’s a bit annoying but it is true in some cases, because I know that my [company 2] placement was because it was a female engineer.
Interviewer: Like positive discrimination?
Subject: Yeah, but then again it’s also – it can work the other way, so I think overall it works out – it’s just a bit unfortunate that people still see it like that (yeah) for it to work in the first place.

Why no macro answer key here

The macro tasks compare survey populations (by placement / university / discipline) and their answer keys are the survey's frozen estimates. The interview arm is women-only and its marginals are volunteer-biased (AGENT_README §5.3/§8), so interview responses are not used as an answer key; the survey arm owns the frozen estimates.
