## Route into engineering — why they chose it, and who influenced them
*Coded variables informed by this theme (8): interest, knowledg, family, mother, careers, teacher, discoura, school*

**Interviewer —** OK so when and why do you think you decided to do engineering?

**Subject —** Er, I didn’t really think about it till I was looking through like prospectuses. It just never really occurred to me, because my school was a bit non-practical and then like career wise they never sort of really mentioned engineering and things like that. They sort of – law and history and things like that and that’s the sort of thing they try and push you into. And so I don’t think it was until after I read the prospectus and then I was reading through and my mum said well, you could do this – I didn’t like anything I was like, I don’t want to do this, it’s sort of worky, too much sitting around reading. My mum was like try engineering, you haven’t read it. I thought, oh that sounds fun, it’s quite me. Sort of you know, practical work basically – so I’m not sitting around, in an office. I get bored and that’s where I came from, really no major reason.

**Interviewer —** So you didn’t really have any careers advice that pointed you in that direction?

**Subject —** I didn’t have a single careers interview through school no, they don’t really mention – like I say they seem to be very, well old school as it were. Oxford, Cambridge – trying to send as many people as they could off to do law and history and English and things.

**Interviewer —** OK, you said that your mum helped you decide on engineering – has your family been quite supportive then of the fact that you…?

**Subject —** Yeah, I mean basically it was I thought about it, thought it was a good idea. I mean my parents weren’t particularly fussed. I mean there were more pleased that I was doing this because it’s vocational. I mean if I’d just sort of gone, I’m going to do English, my mum would have been like, ‘what? What are you going to do afterwards?’ Yeah, so they’re supportive in that, but they’re not really bothered what I do. In fact, it’s more like I choose what I want to do and they’re happy that I’m happy.

**Interviewer —** OK, how did you decide to do civil engineering and not say mechanical or electrical?

**Subject —** That came to me from reading through loads and loads of different university prospectuses and seeing which course I liked the sound of the most. And I liked the sound of civils the most.

**Interviewer —** OK, what do you think it is that does appeal to you about the course?

**Subject —** I think, like I say I think it’s the practical nature of it, because I’m really not just the kind of sit around permanently, type of person. I think that the idea – and actually physically creating something – where we can design it all but then you can actually physically see your work happen, whereas with a lot of other subjects, you know, it’s all up there, it’s all these ideas and theories and you don’t see it happen and I think if you design a building, you know, 4 years later or something, it’s there and you’ll be like, I did that. And I think, I like that.

## Experience of the engineering degree and learning environment
*Coded variables informed by this theme (8): practica, pleased, relevanc, interper, supppers, groupwk, friends, variety*

**Interviewer —** OK, we’ll probably come back to that, after placement and that, a bit later on but I’m going to ask you a few questions about the course itself? Has the course been what you expected it to be?

**Subject —** I don’t really know because I didn’t really come with any expectations as it were. I mean it may sound a bit silly but because I didn’t really know what I was letting myself in for. It seems a little – it’s less practical than I thought it was going to be. I don’t know really how I thought it was going to be more practical but there’s definitely a lot more learning and sort of less actual hands-on than I thought there was going to be but I’m not really – I didn’t really know what to expect when I came so …

**Interviewer —** Yeah, now that you’re here, do you think that this course should be more practical?

**Subject —** Er, it’s not so much about the actual practical stuff, it’s practical applications, you know do certain things and it’s like you sit there and they do it different to like if you go out in the industry, it’s done a different way and you do sort of sit there looking at your lecturers, going you’re telling us all this, but when was the last time you actually went out and you did – on site – something like the surveying equipment. They know how to use it, but we’ve been taught methods and a couple of my friends went out and told them what they’d learnt on site and they were like, that’s ridiculous we don’t do this – we use this way. And while it’s good knowing all the theory you do sometimes – I do sometimes wonder when the last time they applied it in the industry was and they generally speaking, they seem to keep up quite well, so it’s just like the odd 1 or 2 lecturers, you know, sort of think well, you don’t really know.

**Interviewer —** OK, what do you think have been the best and the worse things about the course so far?

**Subject —** Erm, the best I think – I mean I do think there’s a quite a good mix of stuff we learn and as I said the people – I think we’re quite a novel course in that we all get on, as in we’re all actually friends. Whereas a lot of people, it’s not that they don’t get on, they’re just not particularly friendly. Whereas everybody’s really friendly with each other, so you feel you could do any sort of group work and you know you’re gonna have a friend as it were, in your group, you’re not gonna get stuck with people you’re just like, oh my God, who are you? So that’s nice. Erm, the worst bit is there’s just some of the - some of the work we do, you just sit and you’re just like why? Why do I need to know this? Or why are we learning it now? I think we could have spent more time on other stuff that, you know, why have we sort of learnt this? Like some of the water stuff’s been – literally, excuse the pun, but gone straight in at the deep-end and we’re just like why are we learning it, its too technical, why are we learning it now? So, that’s definitely been one of the worse things.

**Interviewer —** The same people?

**Subject —** Well, there’s me an my friend and we fight like cats and dogs on work but its all friendly, as in we’ll bicker and we’ll go on at each other non-stop, but, at the end of the day, we just agree to disagree or something and you know, we’ll walk out the meeting and we’re still best of friends kind of thing and it makes others laugh and we’ll be at each other’s throats but then, at the end of the day, we just went, OK you’re right, I surrender but there’s a couple of people and they’re like really quiet and in a group situation, it drives you mad because I end up being really bossy (yeah) because it’s like if you’ve got work to give out and everyone’s like, I don’t mind, I don’t mind, I don’t mind, I get really frustrated and start dishing out the work because they don’t want to, they’re just sat there going oh it doesn’t bother me. I’m like that to a point, you know, I don’t particularly have a preference, but then if no one’s got a preference I’ll be like, well then, I do have a preference if nobody else does and there’s a couple of people they just don’t speak at all. So you can have a group meeting and they’ll sort of be sat in a corner and like I know they’re thinking, you know, we’ll be putting ideas sort of down on a table and you know they’re there. One of them, if you specifically ask, will then say it to you and that does irritate me, because I know they’re thinking it and you’re like, just say it. It doesn’t matter if it’s stupid, I mean you’re laughing half of my ideas down. You’ve just got to keep – especially, on things like design projects, like ideas for a building, you’ve just to keep producing pictures and it’s again, generally speaking, the people who are very quiet drive you nuts.

**Interviewer —** Do you think that the group work will help you when you get in the industry?

**Subject —** I think it will because, like I’ve said, you learn how to deal, you learn that to sit and shouting with each other doesn’t always work. Sometimes it helps and I get my way but – no, you sort of learn, because you learn important things now, like you learn that sitting, shouting at each other around the table, you learn that you can’t argue you have to learn – you learn when to compromise, I find that you learn sort of when you can and can’t – like you can learn what you can compromise on, on a design or something you can just go OK, then, we’re never going to decide so let’s do this one. I think it’ll help because you’ve got all the – a little bit of fighting out of you for when you’re going and it starts to really matter, in a job.

## The industrial placement — whether and why to go (or not)
*Coded variables informed by this theme (7): placemen, experien, educatio, jobdecid, industry, apptheor, placothe*

**Interviewer —** OK, how did you decide to go on placement then? What things you do think will be good about it?

**Subject —** I think actually having a break in between the years, yes, because I really need the time off and I’ll have chance in the middle as well, to work out if I really want to do civil engineering, because a lot of people are like I don’t want to do it anymore. Well, you don’t know unless you go on site. So, I don’t know, then if you have a cut – a break off, well it’s a year, but then I can finish my degree and look for another job straight away, rather than a – getting a job and getting out if I didn’t like it anymore. Having time in the middle to learn new things, apply things – and you’ll learn more stuff, and when you come – so when you come back you’ll be able to apply it. It’s like me saying things like, ‘cos I was in the lab, doing soils testing, and then this year we started on – we’ve not done any and then we started on soil module and there’s things like they mention as a throw away, like this test and I’d be like oh yeah, I remember that test and I could picture it, what I was doing and then he explained the theory, because I could imagine myself having been doing that, it helped a bit with the theory. So, I think, from personal experience that really helped, so I imagine after doing a year out, you’ll have a lot more experience of things like that and learn a lot more.

**Interviewer —** And why do you think that they decided not to?

**Subject —** Erm, there’s a couple that are older. Erm, there’s a couple that are older and they’ve already done their HND’s and had experience in industry and they’ve been sent by their companies to get a degree (right, OK, yeah). So there’s a couple of them – they’re getting their degrees just to get (yeah) and so they just wanna go straight through and go back out to work. Erm, a couple of us again – there’s a couple that are older and want to get through. Similarly, there’s a couple of people that don’t really have a reason, ‘cos they have been asking them for their reason and they just say, ‘don’t have one’. But, no the majority of people, even I people I know that say they don’t want to do civil engineering as a career are still doing the year out, just because it’s the thing to do sort of thing.

**Interviewer —** Yes. Would you say that one of the reasons you’re thinking of doing it, is to help make your mind up about whether you think it…

**Subject —** Yeah, not least because if I do then want to do civil engineering, I’ll be able to choose within the field of civil engineering. Like when we come back and start choosing modules I’ll be able to be a bit more informed about what I’m choosing and not just go for the easiest modules. I might actually do something I like.

**Interviewer —** OK, what attracted you to the company that you’re going with?

**Subject —** Well, they came and did a presentation here and the guy that did the presentation was really nice and the company sounded – and the company’s quite a big company. Erm, I had an interview with them. Er, obviously they made me an offer. I had an offer from another company as well and the other company was really disorganised and it took them ages to get their letters out, and we only got our offer letters because we said, look we’ve got to apply to another company (right) in a few days – we’ve got to have sent off, so can we please have our offer letters. Again, we got them then – so it was a bit disorganised and when I got – it’s just the letter was very well written – the offer was nice but the letter was really well written; the information was already geared to me. And basically the main reason for choice between them, was because I couldn’t choose between the 2 companies, it basically came down to the letter they wrote. I thought it was nicer.

**Interviewer —** OK what do you think will be the best and the worst things about going on placement?

**Subject —** Best thing I would say is to get experienced a bit and just a break and chance to do something different. Worst thing will probably be the weather in winter and if it’s anything like the summer I worked – the hours, they’re a bit long. Erm, and little things, like silly things I’m worried about, like getting out of the habit of working so when I come back from my year out, suddenly I think Oh God, I have to work again – it will be a nightmare.

## Future career intentions and what matters in a job
*Coded variables informed by this theme (3): carpath, workrole, travel*

**Interviewer —** Do you think that you will be in engineering?

**Subject —** I think I probably will be, because I like it and I like all the ideas of it, so I probably still will be in civil engineering.

**Interviewer —** And do you think that you’d like to go into managing people or do you want to stay on the technical side of things?

**Subject —** I – well I don’t know. Part of that, hopefully, I’ll figure out, on my year out, just by being around people, seeing what they do.

**Interviewer —** Have you thought about doing the consultancy side of it instead of contracting?

**Subject —** Yeah, that side didn’t particularly appeal to me. I’d rather be on the contracting, even if it does mean I have to go outside – I’d rather do contracting than consultancy because you can – on the contracting side you can probably do a bit of both. I know they don’t do that much consulting but some of the companies you can do a bit and it’s – in consultancy, you really can’t do any contracting, so I’d definitely rather be contracting rather than consulting.

**Interviewer —** OK, would you like to work abroad at all, or travel about or …?

**Subject —** Yeah, I would actually. Really like to go and work abroad, but I’m not sure about it.

**Interviewer —** Do you think that generally, the career prospects are good in civil engineering?

**Subject —** I think so because they need them and they need a lot of civil engineers at the minute. I think, plus being a female it – I do think it gives a bit of an edge. If you’re there and they’ve got 2 equally qualified, equally suitable for the job – 1 male, 1 female, you may tip the balance in your favour, just to cover their quota.

**Interviewer —** OK, what about once you’re into the industry? Do you think like the career prospects are good to sort of build and develop your career once you’re…?

**Subject —** I think so, yeah, I think – the only problem in the industry is – it’s a bit hard to have babies and still carry on working, but again then you can move, sort of sideways into an office so sort of come off site and go into an office.
