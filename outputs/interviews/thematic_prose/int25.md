## Route into engineering — why they chose it, and who influenced them
*Coded variables informed by this theme (9): interest, special, mathsci, knowledg, family, varied, careers, discoura, school*

**Interviewer —** OK, when and why then do you think that you decided to do engineering?

**Subject —** I haven’t the foggiest – I haven’t a clue. I always really enjoyed maths at school and it was something that out of all the subjects, it was something I could do – that and physics, you know, really – I enjoyed it most, more than any other subjects and I could do it as well. And also you know, my main ambition is to be an airline pilot and obviously to do that they look at maths and physics quite heavily so, I knew that if that was what I wanted to do, I needed to be good at maths and physics, which was great because I enjoyed it as well and I was quite good at it, so when it came to choosing A levels, it was quite a natural choice really, to get maths and physics, because I needed them and they were my best subjects anyway. But computing was a bit of a (?) – I don’t know why I chose it because I’m awful at computing (laughter) – really bad.

**Interviewer —** Did you ever consider doing maths or physics at university then?

**Subject —** Yeah, I considered it. I mean I didn’t even want to go to University, to be honest. Once I’d finished my A levels I thought right, that’s it, I’ve had enough of education, I’m gonna apply for a pilot’s sponsorship and away I go – how naïve? Er, but obviously before I couldn’t just sort of leave school and do that, I got a job as a Telecom’s analyst for a year, and then as soon as I started sending out applications – September 11th hit and the whole aviation industry went into a slump and I thought, what on earth am I going to do for the next 4/5 years while it picks up, so I decided to go and do a degree ‘cos you know my family wanted me to do it anyway, but I just – I don’t know why, I just didn’t feel drawn to it (yeah) and the more I decided I was going to back to University, obviously I considered maths and physics and everything but erm I’ve always sort of thought well, if I do go to university, I’ll do aeronautical engineering because it kind of combines everything, whereas if you do sort of maths and physics, it’s just a bit hard core really. I think I get enough of it, whereas with engineering you study such a vast range of modules you sort of – it’s a bit of everything, isn’t it? Something to keep you interested. If you were just doing physics, I mean you’d just get really bored with it, so that’s why I chose it.

**Interviewer —** So did you have much careers advice or anything like that at school?

**Subject —** Not really, obviously I had – there was a Careers Officer, we all had those sort of Career interviews in year 10 or whatever. And, it’s all – it sort of gives you a rough idea, you know, you say oh I want to be a pilot or whatever – well go and study computers and maths and physics – right, thanks, bye. It wasn’t – obviously it wasn’t anything too detailed because everyone, you know, you can imagine how many people they’re interviewing and you know, the vast sort of areas they need to go into. But, erm, yeah that was – in high school that was the extent of the careers advice I was given, but when I went to sixth form, I was sort of – I didn’t really know whether I wanted to go to University or not, so I had arranged for another careers interview and he was really good actually. He said, you know, if you did go to University, looking at the subjects you do, you’d probably want to do aeronautical engineering – this is what it entails. Here are the universities that are the best for it, you know, he went into quite a bit of detail, it was quite good.

**Interviewer —** So did anyone particularly encourage you or discourage you from doing engineering?

**Subject —** Er, I knew quite a few people who’d done like aeronautical engineering particularly, who’d gone through it all beforehand and er they were nothing but supportive. The only thing they did say was you know, just be aware of what you’re letting yourself in for, because it is really, really hard – hard work, lots of work, erm but no, I don’t think I received any discouragement from anyone.

**Interviewer —** So do you think that one of the reasons that there might not be more women, it’s not just because it isn’t what they would like but because they don’t know really what it’s about?

**Subject —** Well to be honest, before I came, I really didn’t – I wasn’t that clued about what engineering was about, I knew it was a lot of maths and a lot of physics, so I knew I’d get on with it but I didn’t really understand what it was erm other than when we were at sixth form we went around erm Lancaster University, just to try and get a taste of what was going on. And I went to a talk erm – an engineering talk and you know, I think a lot of people thought engineering was about sort of here, have a spanner and getting greasy and covered in oil and stuff, but that’s a completely different kind of engineer. But I didn’t really know the ins and outs of it. But I think – I think people, when you get to a kind of age when you’re doing you’re A levels, and you’ve shown an interest in maths and physics, I think when it comes to choosing a degree, I think everybody knows that engineering have got to do maths and physics. You know, if you don’t fancy studying maths in great detail or physics in great detail, it’s you know, it’s a bit of both. But I’ve got to be honest, I didn’t really know what it entailed when I came here.

## Experience of the engineering degree and learning environment
*Coded variables informed by this theme (6): curricul, pleased, interper, groupwk, friends, variety*

**Interviewer —** Yes, do you think that group work helps you in that way?

**Subject —** Absolutely, I think it – well, ‘cos when you go into industry that’s what you’re gonna be doing, it’s gonna be sort of mostly team work all the way. So, it’s good that you get to grips with it now and obviously if you’ve got somebody who’s dominating the group or whatever, you’ve got to find a different way of doing what you want to do and it sort of develops, your sort of listening skills and communication skills and that sort of thing. I think it’s essential.

**Interviewer —** OK, do you think there are any particular disadvantages to being a woman engineer?

**Subject —** Er, not – well not that I’ve come across personally, no. I think er – no, I just get treated the same as everyone else really – although, there have been a couple of occasions with certain lecturers, who will remain nameless that I’m there, they just look at you as quite (?) I remember once when I was in the first year we did quite a lot of manufacturing, erm practices – and we had to do some work on waves and because there were only 4 waves, we just went in groups of 4 and it was me and 3 lads, and er the guy taking the session spent the whole time watching me on my wave, you know, with me – like I couldn’t hack it, you know. Thinking the machine was too much for me, and the guy next door to me was having like serious problems and it happened sort of like fairly liquid mixture and it was just seeping out of his machine and he was just – (he wouldn’t leave me alone?) and it ‘s just – and you know, other incidences like it where it’s not the people you’re really, really on the degree with, it tends to be the lecturers who kind of, you know, women engineering, whatever next, kind of thing. The older ones mainly. There’s only a little bit of that, though you know, it’s not every lecturer, just a couple.

**Interviewer —** So, has the course been what you expected it to be?

**Subject —** Yeah, actually, it’s been really interesting. Erm, it’s a bit less to do with aircraft than I thought. At the moment it really is quite a lot of physics and everything and er every once in a while they flash us an aeroplane just hoping and thinking (laughter) kind of thing. But yeah, yeah, it’s interesting. It’s exactly what I though it was – a mixture of maths and physics and – it’s hard – it’s hard work, which I knew it would be hard work and it’s very hour intensive erm but it’s nothing that – you know – it’s everything I thought it would be anyway.

**Interviewer —** What would you say are the best and the worst things about it?

**Subject —** Erm, the best things is – I don’t know, the people you meet. They’re all kind of like minded – everyone does the same thing, there’s a real kind of er – because it’s such a difficult degree there’s a kind of – everybody helps each other, like when we’ve got a really tough piece of course work and everyone’s in there, the day before the deadline, the people who’ve done it, will come over and help out the people who haven’t kind of thing. So you know, it’s a really nice kind of spirit amongst everyone but the down side is just the work load really. We had like 25 hours a week – last semester, which, when you think about when people work they’re doing like 40 hour week, it isn’t very much, but when you think about how much work you’ve got to do in your free time and how tough that work is and how varied it is and in every single module it’s really difficult, that’s quite hard. (Yeah) Interesting though.

## The industrial placement — whether and why to go (or not)
*Coded variables informed by this theme (4): placemen, jobprosp, wkexp, nogain*

**Interviewer —** Have you had to do much group work?

**Subject —** Yeah, quite a bit. Erm, we’ve just done a structural design project – we had to design like a framework originally going for a placement actually. Er, and well lots of reasons really. I mean well my partner has quite a few connections in [company] and he’s – he was an engineer for 3 years when he left Uni – he’s still an engineer now, because he did the same degree as me and then he worked at [place] for 3 years, as like a manual engineer, so he knows that side of it as well. But he’s a pilot now but he still does a lot of engineering on the side so he’s got – he works like on a couple of the aircraft of some [company] managers – so he knows them quite well and er he said to them you know, well – ‘cos I know them as well and he said Jenny’s doing Aeronautical Engineering and I wonder if I could abuse the network and get her an interview kind of thing. And both of them have said, oh you know, oh I know this guy here and he owes me a favour, I know that guy there, as long as she gets a decent degree it shouldn’t be a problem. At the end of the day, the only reason why I’d thought an industrial placement would have been of any value, is for my CV – to improve my employability aspect for working at my degree, but I don’t need that. Which sounds really arrogant but I’ve kind of got these contacts but even if that fell through, I still believe that I could – you know – that if I got an interview, that I could still sort of get my way in there. I don’t believe I need t o take an entire year out just to have this diploma in industry on my CV, purely for one section of employability. I mean the idea would’ve been to have the year out and whether I had the year out, they would probably turn round at the end, had I performed well and said, you know, once you’ve done your degree do you fancy coming back? But I don’t think I need it. That sounds really arrogant but – er, I just though it’s a year out and it’s just a year of – a year I don’t need to waste – I’ve already had a year out in industry, so I’ve been in the real world, I know what it’s about, I’ve worked with – you know – people of all ages. I’ve had to – I mean I’ve been in part time work since I was 13, I don’t really feel the need to spend another year out of my education, working.

**Interviewer —** Do you think if you hadn’t had a year out, after A levels, you might have thought about it?

**Subject —** Er, if I was in the same situation as I am now, probably not, because you know, like I say, even if I hadn’t had the year out – which was brilliant because it was like an office environment, I was working obviously with a team as well. And it was working with people of all ages, all backgrounds and it was invaluable it really was, but I’ve still been, constantly, in part time work since the age of 13 and I think I’ve – even if I hadn’t had the year out, I’d still have really valuable sort of time out in all sorts of places – really diverse places of work, and I’ve met loads of people and you know, obviously I’ve got these sort of contacts here and there and you know, it’s an entire year, which is a long time to improve my employability by a tiny bit really.

**Interviewer —** Do you think there are any other advantages or disadvantages of the placement?

**Subject —** Well really because like – I think if you get a placement with [company], the way I understand it is, they sort of move you about, so once every 2 months you get to spend time in a different department, which is really good because it will give you a better idea of where you – which department – which particular part of aeronautical engineering you really want to go into when you work. But, er I mean that would be invaluable but other than that I can’t really think of anything that would benefit me personally. But other people, who haven’t been out in the workplace, or haven’t had that much experience in working in diverse places with lots of people, would, it would be an invaluable experience.

## Future career intentions and what matters in a job
*Coded variables informed by this theme (7): carpath, workrole, salary, location, people, training, equalopp*

**Interviewer —** Do you like the idea of being able to move round departments?

**Subject —** Yeah, yeah I do. I like the idea of not being pigeon holed to early I like the idea of sort of trying a bit of this and then maybe moving on to a bit of that, after a while and just sort of seeing where I fit in really.

**Interviewer —** OK, is there anything else that you think attracts you to a company – when you start to look for a job do you think you’ll look at things like at training opportunities?

**Subject —** Er, no, not so much training opportunities, that wouldn’t really bother me because I think I’ve had you know, I’ve learnt a lot here and I don’t think they’d put you in charge of anything really important without giving me training or anything. Erm, I don’t – I think Equal Opportunities are getting less and less of an issue these days – personally, although there are some industries where there is a gap between male and females doing the same job – earning different pay, kind of thing. But erm, it – to be honest it wouldn’t really attract me to a company, it would be what areas they specialise in, erm location, salary, salary (laughter) – that’s quite a big pull.

**Interviewer —** What about in 10 years time?

**Subject —** Er, in 10 years time I’d love to be training for my air transport pilot’s licence and making some headway with that ‘cos ultimately that’s where I wanna be and at the end of the day, I’m interested in engineering but it’s not what I want my career – I don’t want to make my career out of it, I just want to get the money – only because it’s £60,000 for an ATPL, which is a lot of years working. And, obviously engineering it’s not that bad these days, so really – it’s sad really but I think it’s a means to an end really but I am interested in it but I don’t – it’s not where I want to spend my career.

**Interviewer —** Because it would involve quite a bit of travelling and…?

**Subject —** Well yeah, different hours and shift work and erratic hours, night stops, yeah it is a big – a big sort of conflict for family life really, especially if your partners doing exactly the same. Yeah, big style. I mean we’ve still got to work that out really. I think my partner really wants to go into the engineering side and he’s doing his – he’s gonna become like a [?] licensed engineer, so I think he’ll go down that road eventually but erm yeah, yeah, well I think whatever you do I think if you’re a woman then you’ve more than likely got the – you’ve got to do the family thing really and you’ve got to be prepared take time out to do that, which is a big barrier and it always will be as far as I’m concerned. I can’t really see any way around it.

**Interviewer —** Are you interested in going into the management side of things or would you like to become more specialised?

**Subject —** I think the more – the higher up in a company you become and you know, the more paperwork’s involved and it’s – I’m sure it comes to the stage where you just spend all your time doing paperwork, so that’s not what I want. I’d much rather be on the shop floor with everyone doing the engineering thing, but, you know, I wouldn’t rule it out altogether. Certainly, a much lower grade manager who’d have control of what was going on but would also dig in as well. That kind of thing I’d be up for.
