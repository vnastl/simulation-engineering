## Route into engineering — why they chose it, and who influenced them
*Coded variables informed by this theme (12): highsal, interest, challeng, family, hobbies, mother, father, careers, teacher, discoura, whodisc, school*

**Interviewer —** So why do you think that you decided to do sound technology?

**Subject —** Basically when I left school I hated academic type work. I hate the fact that you know you read books and in our family, I mean we haven’t got a lot of money so you needed to go into an industry where you could earn a living and have a trade. I loved art in school, but of course you can’t make your money out of doing art really, so music was the obvious choice. And I play, but I just didn’t see a point in getting a certificate saying you can play an instrument, so I thought go into the engineering side.

**Interviewer —** And was your Mum supportive of you doing that then?

**Subject —** On and off. At first when I went to college I was the only girl in the class and I think that shocked her a bit. And then she saw me fiddling with electronics and things and I don’t think she saw that as the kind of thing I should be doing, but since then, when she comes and sees concerts and stuff, she’s been more supportive, when she’s witnessed what I do.

**Interviewer —** Do you think that the type of school had any impact on your decision to do an engineering course at all? Did you have any careers advice or anything like that at school?

**Subject —** No. I mean I know it sounds really sort of cliché, but in girls schools they don’t really advise engineering, which is quite bizarre so. When I said I wanted to go on and do that, I think they were quite shocked and sort of told me how difficult it was going to be and tried to put me off as much as possible from going into engineering. Erm, but as I hated school I was going to do the complete opposite to what they told me anyway, so, that’s when I decided to go and do this.

**Interviewer —** Ok. How did you decide to come to [university] then?

**Subject —** Erm, basically all the music technology courses that are in Britain require you to do playing and they are normally Bas or sometimes BSc’s but you do still have to play and I didn’t want to do that, I wanted to do the complete engineering side of the whole thing. So I needed sound technology and there’s only a few places in Britain that do that and [university] was one of them.

**Interviewer —** When you started doing the BTEC did it concern you that there was a lack of women? Did it ever put you off?

**Subject —** In a way no. In a way it made me want to do it even more. I don’t really get on with girls generally. That sounds really bizarre but I’m more of a sort of boys girl, in the way that you know, you get less bitchiness and it’s a lot more fun. People take what they do a lot more seriously a lot of the time. And so I think in a way when I came in and after getting the first few assignments back, I was one of the youngest and I was getting some of the highest grades, I think I thought ok I’m going to prove every single one of you wrong and do the best that I can, so I did.

## Experience of the engineering degree and learning environment
*Coded variables informed by this theme (10): pleased, relevanc, Mconfid, interper, supplect, supppers, groupwk, friends, coursewk, practwk*

**Interviewer —** Why not?

**Subject —** Mainly course content I think has been a big problem. You know we’re doing sound engineering and instead of spending time doing electronics and engineering based things we’ve been told to go out and make videos, make buttons for webpages, which have absolutely nothing to do with what we’re doing, so you get the feeling its just something to pad out the course content with, which has no benefit to us whatsoever. Whereas they’re lacking in more technical details for it.

**Interviewer —** Do you get loads of coursework and that sort of thing?

**Subject —** Yes we get absolutely … I know the first year I think we got over 35-40 essays, and I mean this year we’ve got so many modules where we’ve got big group projects and when it goes down on paper, you’ve got four essays, but in our case its not because you’ve got studio time, taking the time to do it, sorting out people to get there, so you end up doing four times as much work as anybody else tends to.

**Interviewer —** What would you say has been the best thing about the course then?

**Subject —** Erm, I suppose the people you meet really, that tends to be the best thing wherever you are if I’m honest. I mean obviously you further your knowledge which is good and there are elements that you do love doing like getting in the studios and things, but it’s the tutors you meet are big names, and you get to talk to these people and find out sort of background knowledge and the small things that you know, you can’t learn from theory, you tend to learn from doing. I think that’s been one of the main things, and just the people you work with everyday.

**Interviewer —** Are you pleased that you chose this course then?

**Subject —** I’m pleased I chose the course. I think if I had to do it again, I would probably choose another university to do it in, just because the facilities here really aren’t very good. To try to get into them, you really do have to fight for your time in the studios. But also I mean there’s thing with [university] itself that just don’t function, and they haven’t been functioning. But I suppose what I’d say to that is the year I joined this course, it was the first year they ran the course, so it was a bit of a … we were the guinea pigs, and we’ve been changing things as we’ve been going along, but obviously it doesn’t change for us, it changes for the people coming after us.

## The industrial placement — whether and why to go (or not)
*Coded variables informed by this theme (5): placemen, wkexp, placloca, finuni, noplaoth*

**Interviewer —** Ok, did you have the opportunity to go on a placement at all?

**Subject —** There was a sort of placement year in the third year. The idea was to go away but basically you had to find your own placement, you had to fund yourself. You know the university offered no assistance in doing this. And so it just seemed a bit of a pointless exercise really. You know, I’d rather just get it all out the way. You know it was a lot of things as well, like with friends being here, I want to go through at the same time.

**Interviewer —** It sounds like you’re getting a lot of experience anyway?

**Subject —** Yes, but I could do with a lot more, but it tends to be where you can get your foot in the door at the times you can do it.

**Interviewer —** What do you think are the best and worst things about the work experiences that you have had?

**Subject —** If I’m honest I think I’ve learnt more from working in these situations than I have from sitting in a class and being told what supposedly happens. Because I mean you can be in class and then can tell you, you know, how you sort out a mixing desk say, and then you can get into a live situation and it can be a completely different story. I mean one thing I’ve learnt more from working than from class is communication with people because that’s one thing that they don’t really tend to teach you, but its one of the most important things that you need to be able to handle. Because musicians can be arsey people at the end of the day. You’ve got to try to stress to them without pissing them off what needs to be done and how it needs to be done, so.

## Future career intentions and what matters in a job
*Coded variables informed by this theme (8): carpath, workrole, furstudy, studarea, salary, location, people, travel*

**Interviewer —** Do you know what type of company you’d like to work for when you’ve finished?

**Subject —** Erm, possibly a live company. I like being on the road and sort of meeting new people being in new places. I’d like to do my masters but that’s one of them things that I can’t find funding for so it’s not an option at the minute. But if I did a masters I’d probably do it in stage management so I’m sort of multi-skilled then, in the industry. But generally a touring company or a venue. I wouldn’t like to be in a studio.

**Interviewer —** What do you see yourself doing in say five years time?

**Subject —** Erm, hopefully sort of resident in a venue somewhere. You know somewhere comfortable, just being able to do what I love doing everyday really. Erm, that’s where I’d like to be, whether that’s the case or not are two different things.

**Interviewer —** Would you like to work abroad at all?

**Subject —** Yes, I’d love to go abroad and work. But I think I’d have to, for certain times in the year, you know, for a certain amount of time of the year, I would have to come home. Because, you know, I have family here, I have my friends here. I think if I just upped sticks and moved abroad, I couldn’t do that on a permanent basis. But on a semi-permanent basis, I’d really enjoy doing it.
